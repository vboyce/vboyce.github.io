The audit turned up errors in the code that computed effect sizes and their uncertainty, and an error in the student analysis code for both replications of one study (Ngo et al., 2019). The main result, that 5 of 17 rescue projects (29%) mostly or fully replicated, is unchanged, as are the correlations with predictors (except for one row; see below). With the corrected code, most original effects are statistically consistent with their replications, and the confidence intervals in Figure 1 are wider.

**Analysis errors**

1. *Variance used as a standard error.* The code that computed standardized effect sizes (shared with Boyce et al., 2023) returned the sampling variance of *d* where a standard error was expected. Smaller errors in the same code are also fixed: standard errors for comparisons computed from reported means and SDs, the variance formula for within-participants effects (*d*<sub>z</sub>), and a parsing error in one SD. Together these made the confidence intervals in Figure 1 and the uncertainty used for *p*-original far too small. 1/17 (6%) of the original studies are now statistically inconsistent with their replications (*p*-original < .05; published: 4/17, 24%), with a median *p*-original of 0.27 (published: 0.15). For the secondary comparisons, the proportions with *p*-original < .05 are now 18%, 24%, 12% and 6% (published: 35%, 47%, 35% and 24%).
2. *Effect sizes for Jara-Ettinger et al. (2022).* Effect sizes for this study, coded as a proportion compared with chance, divided by the standard error of the proportion instead of its standard deviation, so they grew with sample size. The corrected effect sizes are 0.76, 0.59 and 0.23 for the original, first replication and rescue (published: 9.12, 6.99 and 4.77). The study now appears in the small-effects panel of Figure 1.
3. *Ngo et al. (2019) replications.* The first replication and the rescue both used the same student analysis code, which subtracted one participant's "independent model" score from a different participant's "data model" score. This left the mean unchanged but inflated the standard deviation about 4- to 6-fold. The values in the paper were taken from the students' reports, so they inherited the error. Reanalysed from the students' posted data with participants aligned, the first replication found the effect, *t*(11) = 3.09, one-sided *p* = .005, *d* = 0.89 (reported: *t*(11) = 0.53; coded *d* = 0.17), and so did the rescue, *t*(76) = 8.40, *d* = 0.96 (reported: *t*(76) = 1.87; coded *d* = 0.10), compared with the original's *d* = 1.28. Ngo et al. (2019) entered the rescue sample because its first replication was scored as a failure, and that score rests on the incorrect analysis. We have kept the subjective replication scores as originally coded (first replication 0, rescue 1), so the 5/17 success rate and the predictor correlations do not change.
4. *Table 2, log original sample size.* This row used the rescue's sample size instead of the original's. Corrected: *r* = .015, *p* = .953 (published: *r* = .027, *p* = .919).

**Text errors and claims that changed**

1. *Statistical consistency.* The Results said that "a number of original effects are not statistically consistent" with the replications and that "the pattern of inconsistency does not align with which studies were rated as having replicated", and the Discussion said that "a large minority" of replications were statistically inconsistent with the original. With one inconsistent study on the primary measure (Tarampi et al., 2016), the Results now say "a few", the second sentence is removed, and the Discussion says "a minority".
2. *Ngo et al. (2019) case study and post-hoc predictors.* The case study said the first replication "failed to find the original effect" and that the rescue "recovered a clear effect (albeit a much smaller one)". We now reference the situation (see analysis error 3)
3. *Schechtman et al. (2010).* The Appendix said the first replication found "a statistically significant effect in the opposite direction of the original study", while Figure 1 shows it in the same direction. The coded effect (the valence-by-distance interaction) was in the same direction as the original in both replications; the reversed result was the overall pattern, with gain-paired tones generalized more than loss-paired tones. The Appendix now says this. In addition, all three Schechtman effect sizes are computed from trial-level regressions (about 70 trials per participant, with no participant-level random effects), so their confidence intervals are too narrow; the first replication's CI excluding 0 depends on this. Its *p*-original values (.66–.88) are far from .05.
4. *Krauss and Wang (2003) rescue.* 1/40 correct justifications was printed as 2%; it is 2.5%.
